# Before you scale, do the math

Many systems do not need fifty servers. They need an order-of-magnitude estimate, made before buying anything. The difference between guessing
and calculating is often a factor of ten, and that factor decides whether the problem is solved with a cache or with a rewrite.

Three laws cover most cases.

## Little's law: concurrency

Little's law says the number of requests in progress equals the throughput multiplied by the time each request stays in the system: **L = λ × W**.

With 200 requests per second and 0.5 seconds of latency, there are on average 100 requests in flight. That number tells you how many threads,
connections or sessions you need, and where they will run out first. If latency rises to two seconds with the same traffic, concurrency quadruples.
Many cascading outages start exactly like this: latency rises, concurrency spikes and resources run out.

## Amdahl's law: the ceiling on parallelism

If part of the work is sequential, adding cores has a limit. With 10 % sequential work, the maximum improvement is ten times, however many cores you buy.
Before parallelising, ask which part can actually be parallelised. Often the bottleneck is a database or a shared lock.

## Queues: latency explodes at the end

When a resource's utilisation approaches 100 %, waiting time grows very fast. That is why a service at 60 % load usually runs well, and one at 95 % suffers
peaks nobody can explain. Traffic variability makes the effect worse. Design with headroom, not with the average.

## A full calculation

Suppose a query service with 200 requests per second at peak, 0.5 seconds average latency and 10 % sequential work. Little tells you that you need capacity
for about 100 simultaneous requests. Amdahl tells you that adding replicas that parallelise the remaining 90 % has a ceiling; if the bottleneck is in the sequential
part, changing the design is worth more than buying machines. Queueing says to keep headroom, for instance keeping utilisation near 70 % at peak as a rule of thumb.

The result is not an exact number but a range with its reasoning. That is what a useful estimate looks like.

## Checklist

- [ ] Do you know the peak throughput, the average latency and the 95th or 99th percentile?
- [ ] How many requests in flight does the system need?
- [ ] Which part of the work is sequential?
- [ ] What maximum utilisation do you accept at peak?
- [ ] Does the decision to scale come from a calculation, or from intuition?
