# Observability: SLIs, SLOs and error budgets

A system can expose a thousand metrics and still nobody knows whether users are fine. CPU and memory describe the
machine; they do not tell you whether a payment went through. Observability exists to answer questions you did not
anticipate when you designed the system, and the first step is deciding which questions matter.

## Logs, metrics and traces

Each signal answers a different question:

- **Logs:** what happened in one specific event, with its context. Useful to investigate a single case.
- **Metrics:** how much, and in which direction. Cheap to store and query, ideal for alerts.
- **Traces:** the path a request took across services and where it spent its time. Essential once there are many hops.

None replaces the others. The usual mistake is collecting logs without any experience metrics, and then spending hours
answering a simple question such as "how many payments are failing right now?"

## SLIs: measuring what the user perceives

A service level indicator (SLI) is a ratio of good events to all events. For example:

- Availability: requests with a valid response, divided by all requests.
- Latency: requests served in under 300 ms, divided by all requests.

Measure at the edge, as close to the user as possible, and along a specific journey such as paying, searching or logging in.
A well-chosen SLI moves when the user suffers, even if the servers look healthy.

## SLOs and the error budget

A service level objective (SLO) sets a target for the SLI over a period. An SLO of 99.9% over 30 days leaves an error budget
of 0.1%, which is roughly 43 minutes of full downtime.

**Worked example.** A payments service receives 10 million requests a month. With a 99.9% SLO it may fail 10,000 of them.
On day 20 (67% of the month) it has 6,200 failures: it has spent 62% of the budget, slightly under the calendar pace. If the
trend holds, it closes the month with about 9,300 failures and some margin left.

The budget turns a debate into a rule. If margin remains, the team can ship risky changes. If it runs out, a policy agreed
in advance gives reliability priority and freezes non-urgent changes.

## Alert on consumption, not on loose symptoms

A useful alert fires when the budget is being spent too fast. The burn rate measures that: if one hour consumes 2% of the
monthly budget, someone should be paged. Using two windows, a short and a long one, avoids alerts on isolated spikes.

## Common mistakes

- **A 100% SLO.** It is unreachable and freezes every change. A realistic target leaves room to deploy.
- **Measuring resources instead of experience.** A CPU at 30% can coexist with a broken checkout.
- **Too many SLOs.** Five indicators per service dilute attention. Start with two or three.
- **Alerts without an action.** If nobody knows what to do when it fires, the alert is noise.

## When it does not apply

A prototype or an internal tool with one user does not need error budgets. Enough logs to debug and a couple of metrics are
enough. A formal SLO is justified only when there are service expectations to meet.

## Checklist

- [ ] Does every important service have an SLI measured at the edge?
- [ ] Is the SLO reachable and agreed with the product owner?
- [ ] Is there a written policy for when the budget runs out?
- [ ] Do alerts fire on budget consumption, with a defined action?
- [ ] Do traces let you follow one request across services?
