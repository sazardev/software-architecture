# Incidents and blameless postmortems

An incident is not the moment to find out who did what; it is the moment to restore the service. And a postmortem is not for
assigning blame; it is for understanding why the system allowed the failure. That distinction decides whether an organization
learns from its outages or repeats them under another name. What we wrote about [observability and SLOs](/docs/observabilidad-slo-presupuesto-error)
tells you when an incident has started; this article is about what comes next.

## Roles during the incident

A badly coordinated incident has too many people touching production at once. The remedy is to separate roles from the start:

- **Incident commander.** Directs the response and sets priorities. The commander does not run commands; their job is to keep the team informed.
- **Operations.** Whoever executes the mitigation: rolling back a deployment, turning off a flag, adding capacity.
- **Communications.** Keeps customers and other areas informed with regular updates, even when there is no news.

## Severities and decisions

Severity must be defined before the incident, with measurable criteria: customer impact, data affected, error budget burned. Each
level triggers a protocol: who is notified, how often updates go out, and when leadership is called. A severity that depends on the
mood of whoever is responding is not useful.

The most useful ordering rule is **mitigate before you understand**. If a rollback restores service in two minutes, do it and
investigate the cause afterwards. Hunting for the root cause while the service is down prolongs the damage.

## A worked example

A payment bug affects 8 percent of transactions for 47 minutes. With 12,000 transactions per hour, that is 960 failures per hour: 752 failed operations in total. Had the commander triggered the rollback at minute 12 instead of minute 47, failures would have stopped at 192. The difference is
not technical; it is procedural. Someone had to have the authority to roll back without waiting until everything was understood.

## The blameless postmortem

A postmortem should answer five questions: what happened, what the impact was, how it was detected, what was done to mitigate it, and what
will change so it does not happen again. Each answer refers to the system: the alert came late, the deployment had no load test, the runbook
did not exist. Writing "so-and-so did not review the change" adds nothing, because the next reviewer will not do better if the process allows it.

John Allspaw popularized this idea as a learning culture: if people fear punishment, they hide mistakes and the system becomes opaque. Sidney
Dekker distinguishes a just culture, which separates honest error from negligence, from a culture of generalized blame.

## Actions with owners and dates

A postmortem without actions is just a document. Each action needs an owner, a date and a way to verify it was completed. It helps to classify
them: those that reduce the likelihood, those that improve detection and those that reduce the impact. A team that only adds alerts for the next
incident learns little; one that removes the source of the failure learns much more.

## Common mistakes

- **Looking for culprits in the meeting.** People stop saying what they did, and the next incident arrives with worse information.
- **Actions without owners.** A task without a person responsible does not get done; it stays on the list forever.
- **Postmortems only for big outages.** Repeated small incidents show patterns that the large ones do not.
- **Ignoring communication.** An informed customer tolerates a failure better than one who finds out on their own.

## When it does not apply

An error with no user impact, fixed in minutes, does not need a full ceremony. A short note with the cause and the action is enough.

## Checklist

- [ ] Are severities defined with measurable criteria before any incident?
- [ ] Does every serious incident have a commander, an operator and a communications owner?
- [ ] Is mitigation decided before the full diagnosis?
- [ ] Is the postmortem written in terms of the system rather than people?
- [ ] Does every action have an owner, a date and a way to verify it?
