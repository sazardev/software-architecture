# What it would cost to leave your cloud

A direct question: how much would it cost to move to another provider tomorrow? If the answer is "I don't know", you have a
dependency nobody decided on. That is not a problem in itself, but it is a decision someone should have made.

## Lock-in is not only the API

The dependency has several layers, and they do not weigh the same:

- **API and programming model:** the proprietary services you use force code rewrites. It is the most visible layer and often
  the cheapest.
- **Data:** exporting data is easy. Rebuilding its meaning, with its relationships, rules and implicit formats, is what costs.
- **Operations:** monitoring, deployment, networking and identity configured to the provider's specifics.
- **Contracts and licences:** commitments on consumption, discounts tied to volume, licences that do not transfer.
- **Talent:** the team knows how to run one provider, not another. That has a cost too.

When someone estimates exit cost by looking only at the API, they are usually wrong by an order of magnitude.

## Estimating it with real data

A useful estimate needs three figures per component: the volume of data to move, the number of integrations that change, and
the acceptable cutover time. With those, the cost stops being an intuition and becomes a range.

The cost also grows over time. Every new service you adopt without thinking widens the dependency. That is why the estimate
should be revisited periodically, not only when the contract is signed.

## Deciding consciously

Not every dependency is bad. A managed service that does not differentiate your business, such as a standard message queue,
may be a commodity worth using. An internal rules engine that is part of your competitive advantage deserves more care. The
question is whether the lock-in you accept sits where it pays off.

## A plan that has been tested

An exit plan is an architecture artefact: it describes the possible destination, the migration sequence, the estimated cost and
the conditions that would trigger it. What turns it into a plan is having executed it at least once, even on a test environment.
A plan that was never run is an intention.

## Checklist

- [ ] Do you know how much leaving would cost, with figures per component?
- [ ] Is each managed service justified as a commodity or as a differentiator?
- [ ] Can the data be exported in an open, complete format?
- [ ] Is there an exit plan, and a date when it was tested?
