# Cloud migration: the six Rs with judgement

"We are moving to the cloud" is not a strategy. It is a direction. Each application in a portfolio needs a different answer:
move it as it is, adapt it a little, rewrite it, replace it with a product, switch it off, or leave it where it is. The six Rs are
a vocabulary for naming those options, popularized by a 2016 AWS article. The value is not in the list but in
the judgement used to assign each application to one of them.

## The six options

- **Rehost.** Move the application to virtual machines in the cloud without changing its code. It is the fastest path and the one
  that takes least advantage of the cloud: the system stays the same, only where it runs changes.
- **Replatform.** Swap one part of the platform for a managed service, such as a managed database, without touching business logic.
  It is the usual middle ground.
- **Refactor.** Redesign the application to take advantage of the cloud, for example by splitting it into components that scale on their
  own. It has the highest value and the highest risk.
- **Repurchase.** Replace the application with a third-party product, such as a CRM instead of an in-house one.
- **Retire.** Switch it off. It is the most underrated option: every retired application stops costing maintenance, security work and time.
- **Retain.** Keep it where it is for now, because moving it does not pay off yet or because dependencies block it.

## Criteria for assigning each application

Four criteria, looked at together, drive the decision: the business value of the application, its current operating cost, the risk of
changing it, and its dependencies. A high-value application that needs heavy change is a refactor candidate; a low-value one nobody uses
should be retired; a medium-value one with an equivalent managed service calls for replatforming.

Dependencies often decide more than the rest. An application that reads data from a system in the data centre cannot move to the cloud without
a strategy for that data; moving it first, without its neighbour, can create latency or transfer costs nobody planned for.

## A worked example

A portfolio of 40 applications. A simple analysis gives this split: 12 rehost, 8 replatform, 4 refactor, 5 repurchase, 6 retire and 5 retain.
The six retirements remove 15 percent of the applications, but their operating cost may be smaller, so the real saving depends on what each one
costs, not on how many there are. A common mistake is to measure the migration by the number of applications moved; what matters is the total
cost and the risk of those that move.

The four refactors concentrate most of the work and the risk. It helps to do them after the fast migrations: by then the team has the platform,
observability and deployment processes working, and design changes rest on firmer ground.

## Why the order matters

Moving the easy things first builds learning and confidence, but it must not leave the hard ones for ever. A reasonable order is: retire what is not
needed, move what does not change, replatform what benefits from managed services, and refactor what justifies it. Starting with the most complex
refactor often stalls the whole programme.

## Common mistakes

- **Rehosting everything out of haste.** You pay for the cloud with the data-centre bill and get no benefit.
- **Forgetting to retire.** A portfolio migrated without retirements keeps applications nobody uses.
- **Assigning without data.** Without a current cost per application, any strategy is an opinion.
- **Treating migration as a project with an end date.** Migrated applications need operation, cost control and governance from day one.

## When it does not apply

For a single small application, a formal analysis of the six Rs is excessive: decide and measure.

## Checklist

- [ ] Is each application's business value, current cost and change risk recorded?
- [ ] Are data and network dependencies identified before anything moves?
- [ ] Have retirement candidates been identified, and have they been retired?
- [ ] Are refactors planned after observability and stable deployments exist?
- [ ] Is there an exit plan, as described in the article on the cost of leaving the cloud?
