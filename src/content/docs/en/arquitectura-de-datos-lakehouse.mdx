# Data architecture: lakehouse and data mesh

For years, analytics architecture was simple to describe. Applications wrote to their operational
databases, a nightly job copied the data into a data warehouse, and reports read from there. That
worked while the data was structured and the consumers were few. Two things have changed: data science
teams need files, text and images, and every extra copy adds latency, cost and places where the data
can disagree.

Three patterns compete to solve this: the classic warehouse, the lakehouse and the data mesh. They are
not mutually exclusive, but each one forces different decisions.

## The warehouse: when it is enough

A warehouse with a fixed schema, dimensional modelling and SQL queries is the most mature option. It is
the right choice when the data is structured, reports are the main consumer and the team is small. Its
limit shows up when the same data must also feed a machine learning model. The model needs files and
full histories, and the warehouse becomes one more copy to maintain.

## The lakehouse: one storage, several consumers

A lakehouse keeps data in cheap object storage, as columnar files, and adds an open table layer on top,
such as Delta Lake, Apache Iceberg or Apache Hudi. That layer provides transactions, schema evolution and
time travel over files that any engine can read. The same dataset serves SQL for reports and Python for
models.

The benefit is avoiding copies. The risk is that the lake turns into a swamp if nobody governs the
catalogue, the permissions or the quality. A lakehouse without a catalogue and without data contracts is
just cheap storage with data nobody trusts.

## The data mesh: the domain as owner

The data mesh, proposed by Zhamak Dehghani, is an organizational model rather than a technology. Each
business domain publishes its data as a product, with an owner, measured quality and an explicit contract.
A shared platform provides the tooling. It makes sense when there are many domains and a central data team
has become the bottleneck. Without a mature platform and owners willing to answer for their data, the mesh
becomes silos with a new name.

## A worked example

A company with four domains (orders, payments, shipping and customers) and 40 TB of events per month needs
daily reports and a payment-risk model retrained every week. With a warehouse, the model would require
exporting about 40 TB a month to a second system. With a lakehouse, both read the same open table: storage
stays cheap and there is no copy to synchronise. If the company also has 30 independent data teams, each
domain should own its published tables, with the platform as the common base.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing a data mesh before there is a platform that teams can use without help.
- Building a lakehouse without a catalogue, access control or measured table quality.
- Keeping the same data in the warehouse and the lake out of habit.

## When it does not apply

If your analytics are ten weekly reports and no models, a managed warehouse is enough and cheaper to run.
The lakehouse and the mesh solve problems of scale and organization. Without those problems, they only
add complexity.

## Checklist

- [ ] Which consumers need the data: reports, models or both?
- [ ] Is there a catalogue with owners and permissions per table?
- [ ] Does every published table have a contract and a quality measure?
- [ ] How many domains produce data, and how many teams consume it?
