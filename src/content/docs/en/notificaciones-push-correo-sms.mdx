# Notifications: push, email and SMS reliably

A notification looks trivial: an event happens and the user gets a message. In practice it mixes three hard
problems. Each channel has its own provider and failure modes, users have preferences that must be respected,
and retries can turn one message into ten. If the logic lives inside every business service, the result is
duplicated and chaotic.

## A decoupled service

The sensible architecture separates two things. Business services publish events ("order shipped"), and a
notification service decides what to send, to whom and through which channel. The producer does not know
whether the user has push enabled or which provider handles email.

That service checks the user's preferences and consent, and queues one message per channel. One queue per
channel isolates failures: if the SMS provider goes down, the SMS queue grows while email keeps flowing.

## A worked example

Suppose one million users receive two notifications a day, and 30% of them go by SMS. The total volume is
2 million a day, about 23 per second on average. SMS accounts for 600,000 a day, about 7 per second.

If the SMS provider is down for two hours, the queue accumulates 7 × 7,200 seconds, about 50,000 messages.
When the provider returns, those messages must go out at a rate the provider tolerates, not all at once. That
calculation decides how much resend capacity to reserve.

## Preferences and consent

Preferences should live in one place, with consent stored as separate, dated data. A user who withdrew consent
for marketing must not receive a promotion, even if their email address is valid. This check runs before
queueing, not after, because a message already sent cannot be recalled.

## Deduplication and frequency limits

An event can arrive twice, and a retry can repeat a send that did go through. Deduplication uses an
idempotency key per notification and per channel, stored for a period that covers the retries. Frequency
limits, such as at most three messages a day per user, are enforced at the same point so that no channel can
bypass them.

## Retries that do not lose messages

Each send can fail transiently. Retries use exponential backoff with jitter, so they do not bunch together.
After several attempts the message moves to a dead-letter queue for review instead of disappearing silently.
No message should be left without a state: it is delivered, retrying or under review.

A send may have been accepted even when the provider's response arrives late or is lost. So the retry should
reuse the same idempotency key, if the provider supports it, and the message state must be updated with the
identifier the provider returns. Without idempotency support, delivery is at least once: duplicates are possible
and must be accepted as a risk.

## Common mistakes

- Sending from the business service and repeating the preference logic in each one.
- A single queue for all channels, so that one slow channel stalls the rest.
- Retrying without a limit or jitter, and saturating the provider when it recovers.
- Discarding failed messages without recording them.

## When it does not apply

If the application sends one kind of email with no complex preferences, a module inside the business service
is enough. The dedicated service is justified when there are several channels, several producers and consent
requirements.

## Checklist

- [ ] Do business services only publish events, without choosing the channel?
- [ ] Does each channel have its own queue and resend capacity?
- [ ] Is consent checked before queueing?
- [ ] Does every notification carry an idempotency key?
- [ ] Are failed messages recorded for review?
