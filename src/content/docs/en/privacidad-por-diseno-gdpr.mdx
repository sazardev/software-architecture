# Privacy by design: data, retention and deletion

Privacy usually arrives late. A team builds the product, keeps everything "just in case", and when someone asks
for their data to be deleted, it discovers copies in ten places. The European General Data Protection Regulation
(GDPR) anticipates this in Article 25: data protection must be built in by design and by default, so that only
the data needed is processed. It is an architecture requirement, not paperwork.

## Four decisions that change the design

The first is **minimization**. The principle in Article 5 asks that data be adequate, relevant and limited to what
is necessary. If the shipping service does not need a date of birth, it should never receive one. Every field you
do not collect is a field you do not have to protect, encrypt, back up or delete.

The second is **legal basis and consent**. Consent must be demonstrable and as easy to withdraw as it was to give.
That is why it is worth storing as data: what was accepted, when, under which text version, and for which purpose.
Consent that lives only in application code is not evidence.

The third is **retention with periods**. Each category of data needs a retention period and a reason. Billing records
may have a legal period; navigation logs usually need a much shorter one. A period that nobody enforces is the same
as no period at all.

The fourth is **deletion that reaches everywhere**. Article 17 grants the right to erasure, but data lives in the main
database, in backups, in logs, in analytics and in caches. If deletion only touches the first, the right is not met.
You need an inventory of where each personal data item lives and a process that walks that inventory.

## A worked example

A platform has 200,000 users who ask to be deleted in one year. If each request touches five stores, that is one
million deletion operations. If the process is manual and each takes 10 minutes, that is 166,667 hours, roughly
83 person-years at 2,000 hours per person. With an inventory and an automated job that walks the five stores, each
request takes minutes and the result is verified monthly. The difference is not only compliance; it is feasibility.

Backups deserve their own note. Deleting from a backup is expensive, so the usual approach is to document when
the data expires as backups rotate, and, if a restore is needed, to reapply the pending deletions afterwards.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating privacy as a document.** A privacy policy protects nothing if the system keeps everything.
- **Forgetting logs.** A personal data item written into an error log survives any deletion from the database.
- **Confusing encryption with anonymization.** Encrypted data remains personal while the key exists.
- **Having no inventory.** If you do not know where each item lives, deletion is an empty promise.

## When it does not apply

If the system does not process personal data, these decisions are not needed. They also do not replace legal advice:
the scope of the legal basis and the exact periods depend on the case and the jurisdiction.

## Checklist

- [ ] Does every personal data field have a documented purpose?
- [ ] Is consent stored as data, with version and date?
- [ ] Does each data category have a retention period enforced by a process?
- [ ] Is there an inventory of every store holding personal data, including logs and backups?
- [ ] Is deletion verified and recorded?
