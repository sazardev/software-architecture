# Your org chart draws your architecture

In 1968, Melvin Conway observed that organizations design systems that copy their communication structure. It was
not a joke: three teams that rarely talk to each other produce three big pieces with awkward interfaces between them,
even though nobody designed them that way.

The practical consequence is uncomfortable. If your org chart does not change, your architecture will not either. And
if every change goes through five teams, your system will have five coordination points, however the diagram labels them.

## What it looks like in a project

Imagine a payment flow that has to touch the billing team, the gateway team and the notifications team. If each one
has its own backlog and its own deadlines, the flow becomes three components with contracts negotiated in meetings.
Every change to the flow needs three coordinated deployments, and nobody owns the complete experience.

That structure is nobody's fault. It is the logical result of how responsibilities are split.

## The inverse maneuver

The inverse Conway maneuver means doing the opposite: design the value flow you want first, then the teams that sustain
it. If you want an independent payments service, create a team that owns that flow end to end, and let the architecture
follow.

_Team Topologies_ offers a vocabulary for this:

- **Four team types:** stream-aligned, platform, enabling and complicated-subsystem.
- **Three interaction modes:** collaboration, X-as-a-Service and facilitation.

The team type says what responsibility a team carries, and the mode says how it relates to others. A platform team that
behaves like a ticket queue is not doing its job, however its name reads.

## Cognitive load

One limit is often forgotten: a team can only hold so much domain. If a team owns too many subsystems, it will understand
none of them well, and the architecture will decay in the parts nobody looks at.

Before creating a team, ask whether it can understand what it is going to own. If not, the answer is not to add people by
force, but to split the domain into smaller boundaries.

## Checklist

- [ ] Can you draw your org chart and predict the shape of your architecture?
- [ ] Is there one team that owns each important value flow end to end?
- [ ] How many teams touch the same change? Each one is a coordination point.
- [ ] Is each team's cognitive load reasonable for the domain it owns?
