# Monorepo or polyrepo: the decision and its costs

Most organizations reach this question without having decided it: each service has its own repository because the
first one was created that way. When a change needs five repositories, the polyrepo becomes a coordination problem.
When a repository takes twenty minutes to clone, the monorepo becomes another one.

The choice is not between fashion and tradition. It is between two ways of paying for coordinating changes, versioning
and building.

## What each option means

In a **polyrepo**, each package or service has its own repository, version and release cycle. Consumers update when they
choose, which gives autonomy but lets versions drift apart. In a **monorepo**, many packages live in the same repository,
are built together, and a change that touches several of them lands in a single atomic commit.

They are not two ends of one scale: a monorepo can have very clear internal boundaries, and a polyrepo can share a common
template. What differs is where the coupling lives.

## When a monorepo pays off

A monorepo pays off when changes cross many packages often, when internal libraries must stay on one coherent version,
and when an incremental build tool can run only what is affected. Google described this model in 2016: one very large
repository, with tooling that makes working in it feasible.

Its main advantage is that a cross-cutting refactor lands completely or not at all. The trade-off is that CI, code search
and permissions must scale with the repository.

## When a polyrepo pays off

A polyrepo pays off when teams are independent, when each service has its own lifecycle and risk level, or when access
controls must separate repositories by team or by customer. Its advantage is autonomy. Its cost is coordination: a contract
change needs several pull requests, version bumps and waiting for each consumer to respond.

## A worked example

A team has 9 services and 3 shared libraries. Last quarter, 1 in 4 changes had to touch at least two repositories, and each
contract change took 6 days on average to reach production, against 1 day when the change fitted in one repository. An illustrative
estimate: if a monorepo halves those cross-cutting changes, and incremental builds cut CI from 12 minutes to 4, the tooling
investment could pay back within a few months. If instead the 9 services deploy on independent cycles and rarely touch each other,
the polyrepo remains the cheaper option.

## Hidden costs

- **Monorepo:** CI needs incremental builds and caching, or every commit takes too long. Fine-grained permissions cost more.
  A failure in a shared dependency can block everyone.
- **Polyrepo:** versions misaligned across services, copied templates that diverge, and security tooling to configure in
  every repository.

## Common mistakes

- Moving to a monorepo without first investing in incremental builds.
- Choosing a polyrepo by default and paying for coordination for years without measuring it.
- Treating the decision as permanent. It can be changed, but the change is expensive.

## When it does not apply

If the team is small and every service deploys together, either option works. The decision matters when size, the frequency
of cross-cutting changes or access requirements start to create friction.

## Checklist

- [ ] What share of changes touches more than one package?
- [ ] How long does CI take today for a small change, and how long would it take with incremental builds?
- [ ] Do permissions need to be separated by repository?
- [ ] Is the decision recorded in an ADR, with criteria for review?
