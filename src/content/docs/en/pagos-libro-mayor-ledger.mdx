# Payments and the ledger: double entry without errors

A charge looks simple: the customer pays, the system records it. But once refunds, fees, taxes, partial payments
and retries appear, the balance of an account depends on the order and the number of operations applied. The way to
keep it correct is old: double-entry bookkeeping, described by Luca Pacioli in 1494, and a ledger that is written
only through postings.

## Immutable postings

In double entry, every posting balances: its debits and credits add up to the same amount. A charge of 100 euros is one posting
with cash debited 100 and revenue credited 100. If a posting is wrong, it is not edited: it is cancelled by a
**reversal** that inverts the amounts, and the correct posting is recorded after it. The original stays.

This changes the design. The ledger table is append-only; there is no `UPDATE` on amounts. Any balance is computed by
summing postings, or kept as a projection that can be rebuilt from the history. The full history is the evidence of
what happened and in which order, which is what an audit asks for.

## Idempotent charges

A charge can arrive twice: the customer retries, the provider resends a webhook, or a worker crashes after confirming
the charge and before writing the posting. So every operation with an external effect carries an idempotency key,
stored with the posting it produced. If the key already exists, the answer is the previous result, with no second
posting and no second charge.

## Reconciliation: the ledger against the provider

The internal ledger is never the only source of truth. The payment provider keeps its own record, with its fees, its
settlement delays and its refunds. Reconciliation compares both every day: each provider charge must have a posting,
each posting must have a charge, and differences go to a review queue with an owner.

## A worked example

An order of 100 euros with a 3 percent fee (3 euros). The provider settles 97 euros. The postings are: cash 97 debit,
bank fees 3 debit, revenue 100 credit. If the customer asks for a full refund of 100 euros, a reversal is recorded: revenue 100 debit, cash 100 credit. Cash now shows minus 3 euros: that is the fee the provider does not return, and it stays as an expense. If the refund posting had been 97 instead of 100, reconciliation would flag a 3-euro difference on the settlement date.

## Event order

Payment events can arrive out of order: a refund before the charge, or a late confirmation. The ledger must tolerate
it. Reasonable rules: a refund can only reverse existing charges, every posting references the operation that caused it,
and state transitions are explicit. An out-of-order event is queued until its dependencies exist, rather than applied blindly.

## Common mistakes

- **Editing amounts.** A correction on an existing posting destroys the audit trail.
- **Trusting the webhook.** Without reconciliation, one lost event is a wrong balance forever.
- **Mixing currency and rounding.** Store amounts in the smallest currency unit, not in floating-point decimals.
- **Confusing status with postings.** "Paid" is an order status; money moves through postings.

## When it does not apply

For a shop with simple charges and no refunds or complex taxes, a transactions table with idempotency may be enough.
A ledger pays off when there are accounting obligations, several providers, or a need to audit every cent.

## Checklist

- [ ] Does every posting balance, with debits equal to credits?
- [ ] Are corrections reversals, and is the history append-only?
- [ ] Does every operation with an external effect have an idempotency key stored with its posting?
- [ ] Is there a daily reconciliation with the provider, with an owner for differences?
- [ ] Are amounts stored in the smallest unit of the currency?
