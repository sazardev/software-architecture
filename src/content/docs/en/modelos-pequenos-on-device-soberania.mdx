# Small models, on-device and data sovereignty

By default, most teams call the API of a large frontier model. It is the most convenient
option: there is no infrastructure to run and quality is high. It is not always the right one.
A small language model (SLM) that runs on a phone, on your own server or inside the
organization's environment can be better for certain tasks, and it also changes where the data
lives. This article covers when that choice pays off, what it costs, and how to combine both
worlds without losing control.

The thesis is simple: **a small model does not compete on general intelligence, it competes on
boundaries**. It wins when the task is narrow, the data must stay inside a perimeter, or the
answer has to arrive in milliseconds without a network. It loses when the task needs broad
reasoning or when you want quality without maintenance effort.

## What we mean by "small" and "on-device"

"Small" is relative and has no official threshold. It is measured in parameters, but what
matters to the architect is whether the model fits in the available memory and answers within
the latency budget. It helps to separate three places where a model can run, because each gives
different guarantees:

- **Frontier API:** the provider operates the model. Your data travels to their infrastructure,
  and you depend on their availability, prices and versions.
- **Your own infrastructure:** the model runs on your servers, in a private cloud or with a
  provider that offers controlled residency. You decide region, retention and logs.
- **On the device:** the model runs on the phone, the laptop or the browser. The data never
  leaves the device, unless the app sends it on purpose.

They are not mutually exclusive. One product can use all three depending on the task. The
architecture question is which task goes where, and why.

## The decision criteria

Before choosing a small model, you should be able to explain which problem it solves that the
API does not. There are six criteria, and it is worth evaluating them in this order.

**Latency.** A local answer does not depend on the network or on the provider's queue. For
autocomplete, per-keystroke classification or live corrections, that difference decides the
experience. Note that total latency includes loading the model into memory, which on a device
can be the slowest part of the first use.

**Privacy and residency.** If the data does not leave the perimeter, several contract and
transfer questions disappear. The obligations do not: the data is still personal, it still
needs a legal basis and retention, and the local model can also write the data to logs or
caches. See [privacy by design](/docs/privacidad-por-diseno-gdpr).

**Cost at volume.** With little traffic, the API is almost always cheaper because you pay for no
idle infrastructure. With heavy traffic and repetitive tasks, the calculation can flip: the cost
becomes compute and operations instead of per-token billing. The breakdown of what makes up
that cost is in [inference cost](/docs/costo-de-inferencia). What matters is measuring with your
own volume, not with catalogue figures.

**Offline availability.** An app that must work on the subway, on a factory floor or on a ship
cannot depend on an HTTP call at every step. It is the same problem as
[offline-first](/docs/movil-offline-first-sincronizacion) for data: the model ships with the app
and synchronization, if any, happens afterwards.

**Version control.** With your own model you fix the version and decide when to change it. A
provider can retire a model or change its behavior. In regulated processes, being able to
reproduce an answer from six months ago is a requirement, not a convenience.

**Measurable quality.** You only know whether the small model is enough if you have your own
evaluations. Without them, any choice is a matter of faith. See
[continuous evaluation](/docs/controlar-la-ia-gobierno-y-autonomia#continuous-evaluation).

## What you give up

Choosing a small model means accepting costs that the API hides. Write them down in the
decision:

- **Reasoning capacity.** Open-ended tasks, long multi-step chains and ambiguous instructions
  usually degrade first in small models. The failure is not always obvious: the answer reads
  well and is wrong.
- **Maintenance.** You load the model, update it, watch memory, manage hardware acceleration and
  fix compatibility errors. That work needs an owner.
- **Updates.** Advances reach the large models first. A pinned model is stable, but it ages; you
  need to plan when to re-evaluate it.
- **Your own evals.** Without a set of real, labeled cases run on every change, you cannot tell
  whether an update made something worse.

## Hardware as a real constraint

The architecture of a local model starts with the hardware, not the model. Three limits dominate
almost every decision:

1. **Memory.** The weights and the context cache must fit in RAM or accelerator memory. If they
   do not, the system degrades or crashes. It is the constraint that weighs most on a phone
   shared with other apps.
2. **Energy and heat.** Sustained inference drains the battery and throttles the device after a
   few minutes. A background task must be measured in consumption, not only in latency.
3. **Accelerators.** The NPU, GPU or accelerator of a machine changes the result a lot, and not
   every device has one. A design that only works on the latest phone model leaves part of your
   users out.

**Quantization** is the usual lever to reduce memory: storing weights with fewer bits. It is a
trade-off, not a free improvement. Fewer bits reduce memory and sometimes speed, but they degrade
quality unevenly: some tasks hold up and others do not. The only way to choose the quantization
level is to measure quality on your task with your data. Formats and levels depend on the
inference tool you use, so that tool's documentation takes precedence over any generic table.

Mobile and system platforms already ship built-in models and frameworks to use them. Apple
documents its system model framework at
[developer.apple.com](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/foundationmodels), including its
context and usage limits. Before designing on top of one of these models, check the current
documentation: capabilities change with each system release.

## Open models: what "open" means

"Open model" is used for different things, and the difference affects what you can legally do.

In 2024 the Open Source Initiative published the **Open Source AI Definition 1.0**. Under it, an
AI system is open when its parameters are released under OSI-approved terms, its training and
running code under an approved license, and there is enough information about the data to build
a substantially equivalent system. A model that only publishes its weights under a custom license
does not meet that definition, even if the catalogue calls it "open"
([OSI](https://opensource.org/press-mentions/osi-officially-releases-its-definition-for-open-source-ai)).

In practice there are three situations:

- **Weights under a permissive license** (for example Apache 2.0 or MIT): you can use, modify and
  distribute them with few conditions. Still check the exact license of each version.
- **Weights under a restricted community license.** Some licenses from well-known families
  include acceptable-use clauses and size thresholds. The Llama 3 license, for example, requires
  asking Meta for a license if the products or services of the licensee and its affiliates had more
  than 700 million monthly active users in the preceding calendar month, measured at the Llama 3
  release date (18 April 2024), and subjects use to Meta's acceptable use policy (read the official
  text for the version you use). For a
  small company the threshold is irrelevant; for another it may be decisive.
- **No clear license or research-only terms.** It is not a production model, however well it
  performs in a demo.

For the architect, the concrete question is: can I serve this model in my product, to my
customers, in my country, with the obligations its license imposes? That answer should be written
down next to the version you chose. An open model also has provenance: knowing what data trained
it is part of the risk assessment, even when its license allows use.

## Data sovereignty: where it is processed and where it is stored

Data sovereignty is the ability to say, precisely, which jurisdiction processes and stores the
data, who can access it and under which law. With a model behind an API, the contract and the
provider's documentation give that answer. With a local model, you give it.

There are at least four places where control is lost without anyone noticing:

- **Logs and traces.** LLM observability tools often store prompts and answers. If personal data
  reaches the logs, the perimeter extends to the logging platform.
- **Caches.** A semantic or prompt cache can keep data outside the intended location.
- **Client telemetry.** Some tools send metrics or usage fragments to the software vendor, even
  when the model is local.
- **Backups and replicas.** What is copied for recovery is also processing.

In Europe, transfers of personal data outside the European Economic Area are governed by
Chapter V of the [GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj). The EU-US Data Privacy
Framework, adopted by the Commission as an adequacy decision in 2023 (Implementing Decision (EU)
2023/1795), is one of the bases for those transfers. Adequacy decisions are reviewed periodically
and their continuity depends on the legal framework of the receiving country, so do not assume it
is in force indefinitely: check its current status with the European Commission before deciding.
That is not a reason to change provider outright, but it is a reason not to build the whole system
on a single transfer basis and to have an exit plan. Some sectors also have their own residency or
supervision rules, which may require local infrastructure regardless of what the GDPR says.

A practical rule: if you cannot say in one sentence where each copy of the data your model
receives lives, your sovereignty is only theoretical.

## Routing: simple locally, hard elsewhere

Many systems do not choose between a small and a large model; they combine them. A **router**
decides per request: the simple cases are solved by the local model and the hard ones go to the
frontier API. The pattern reduces cost and latency and, done well, protects the most sensitive
data.

Academic work has measured it. RouteLLM (Ong et al., ICLR 2025) trains routers on human
preference data between a strong and a weak model, and reports cost savings of more than 2x with
no loss in quality on its evaluations, with some ability to generalize to models not seen in
training ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18665),
[ICLR 2025](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2025/poster/30737)). These are results on their benchmarks;
your traffic may give a different figure.

The pattern has concrete risks:

- **The router also processes data.** If the difficulty classifier relies on an external model,
  you have already sent the data out before deciding. The router must live inside the same
  perimeter as the most sensitive data.
- **Classification errors.** A hard task sent to the small model produces plausible but wrong
  answers. Measure false negatives, not only the savings.
- **Two behaviors.** Different users get answers of different quality depending on the route.
  That complicates support, testing and explanations.
- **Hidden cost.** Maintaining two models, two sets of evals and routing logic takes work. In a
  small system it may not pay off.

## When yes and when no

A small model makes sense when:

- the task is narrow (classify, extract fields, rewrite, summarize a short text) and you have
  real cases to measure it;
- personal or confidential data must not leave a specific perimeter;
- latency or offline operation are product requirements;
- volume is high and repetitive, and you already have the capacity to operate infrastructure;
- you need to pin a version and reproduce results.

Staying with a frontier API makes sense when:

- the task requires broad reasoning or multi-step work with tools;
- volume is low and you do not want to operate anything;
- you have no evaluations of your own and do not plan to build them;
- the license of the local model does not cover your use or your scale.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing by size.** "It is small, so it will be cheap and fast" without measuring quality on
  your task or real latency on the target device.
- **Confusing local with private.** A model on the phone does not protect data that is later sent
  to analytics, logs or an external service.
- **Ignoring the license.** Discovering it when scaling, once there are customers in a specific
  market.
- **Quantizing without measuring.** Applying the lowest level that fits in memory without checking
  which tasks get worse.
- **Routing without evaluating the router.** Assuming the classifier is right and not measuring the
  hard cases that stay on the small model.
- **Pinning the model forever.** Not planning when to re-evaluate it or what would change the
  decision.

## Checklist

- [ ] The task is narrow and has a set of real cases to evaluate it.
- [ ] There is an explicit reason (latency, residency, cost, offline, version) not to use the API.
- [ ] You know on which devices or servers it runs and what memory and acceleration it needs.
- [ ] The model license allows your use, your scale and your markets, and it is documented.
- [ ] You have mapped where the data lives: input, cache, logs, telemetry, backups.
- [ ] If there is an international transfer, you have a legal basis and a fallback plan.
- [ ] If you route, the router lives inside the perimeter and you measure false negatives.
- [ ] You pin the model version and have a schedule to re-evaluate it.
