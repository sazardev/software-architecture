# The architect as facilitator

Some architects decide everything, and others decide nothing. The first kind creates dependence: the team waits for
approval on every change. The second kind creates a vacuum: nobody knows who answers when a technical conflict comes up.
Neither extreme works.

An architect who influences well has technical authority without formal authority. The job is to make sure the decisions
are good and that the people who should make them do. That requires knowing when to step in and when to hold back.

## Influencing without formal authority

In most teams the architect has no direct reports and no budget. Influence comes from the quality of the analysis, the
trust built by listening and the clarity with which decisions are documented. Gregor Hohpe compares the role to an elevator
that carries information between the engineering floor and the executive floor.

This changes how the work gets done. Instead of imposing a solution, the architect asks questions that force criteria into
the open, and lets the team reach its own conclusion.

## Decide, consult or delegate

Not every decision deserves the same treatment. In 1958, Robert Tannenbaum and Warren Schmidt described a continuum between
leadership centred on the boss and leadership centred on the group. Adapted to the architect, it has three points:

- **Decide.** When the decision is costly to reverse, affects several teams, or carries a security or compliance risk the
  team cannot assess on its own.
- **Consult.** When the team has the context, but the architect adds criteria or experience. This is usually the most common point
  and the hardest to do well, because it requires real listening before any opinion is given.
- **Delegate.** When the decision is reversible, has local impact and the team has the information it needs.

The control question is simple: what happens if we get it wrong, and how expensive is it to reverse? If the answer is "not
much", delegate. If it is "a lot", decide or consult more deeply.

## A worked example

A team wants to change how its public API paginates. The options are cursor-based and page-based pagination. The architect
knows that page-based pagination makes consistency harder when the data changes, but the team knows its clients far better.

The architect asks the team to write down the real use cases, evaluates the cost of each option against them and proposes a
decision criterion. The team chooses cursors, because its clients use infinite scrolling. The architect records the decision
in an ADR and does not reopen it unless the context changes. The team understands the reasoning and defends it as its own.

## Common mistakes

- **Becoming the bottleneck.** If every decision passes through you, the team learns to wait instead of thinking.
- **Consulting as a formality.** Asking for an opinion and then ignoring it erodes trust faster than not asking.
- **Staying silent about serious issues.** Delegating does not mean stop warning. A technical disagreement is stated clearly,
  and a serious risk is escalated.

## Psychological safety

None of this works if the team is afraid of getting things wrong. Amy Edmondson defines psychological safety as a shared belief
that the team is a safe place for interpersonal risk-taking, such as admitting a mistake or asking an awkward question. Without
that safety, consultations become formal and problems surface late, in production.

The architect contributes in concrete ways: thanking people for objections, admitting their own design mistakes and separating
criticism of a proposal from criticism of the person who made it.

## When it does not apply

In a new team, deciding more than usual is reasonable for a while, handing over ground as soon as possible. During an active
incident, direct coordination beats facilitation. What should not be flexible is the goal that the team can decide without you.

## Checklist

- [ ] Do you know which decisions are yours and which belong to the team?
- [ ] Does consulting include listening before giving an opinion?
- [ ] Is each important decision recorded together with its reasoning?
- [ ] Are disagreements stated in a way the team can discuss?
- [ ] Does the team decide without you in the reversible cases?
