# Authorization at scale: RBAC, ABAC and ReBAC

Authentication answers "who are you?". Authorization answers "may you do this?", and it is the question asked most often
in any system: every read, every write, every button. In a small application, one check in the code is enough. At scale,
the same rule appears in twenty services, each with its own copy, and a policy change takes weeks to reach all of them.

Three classic models organize the problem: role-based access control (RBAC), attribute-based access control (ABAC) and
relationship-based access control (ReBAC). Each one takes a different thing as its source of truth.

## RBAC: the role as the source of truth

In RBAC, each person is assigned roles (administrator, editor, reader) and each role groups permissions. It is the easiest
model to understand and to audit: the question "who may delete invoices?" is answered by looking at which roles hold that permission.

Its limit appears with exceptions. "Editor, but only in their region" or "editor, except for confidential documents" forces
a new role for every combination, and the number of roles grows out of control. This is known as _role explosion_.

## ABAC: attributes as the source of truth

In ABAC, the decision depends on attributes of the user, the resource, the action and the context: region, department,
document classification, time of day. A policy might say that a user may read a report if their department matches the
report's and the report is not classified as confidential.

ABAC expresses fine-grained rules well, but it requires attributes to be reliable and available at the moment of deciding.
If the department attribute is stale in one service, the decision is wrong and nobody notices. NIST published a reference
guide for ABAC in 2014 (SP 800-162).

## ReBAC: relationships as the source of truth

In ReBAC, the decision follows the graph of relationships between people and resources: X owns a document, the document
belongs to a folder, Y is a member of a team that has access to that folder. Permissions are derived by walking those relationships.

It is the natural model for sharing: documents, projects or resources with hierarchies. In 2019 Google described Zanzibar, its
system that applies this approach at large scale with global consistency. Its cost is the graph itself: it needs storage,
indexes and a way to answer with enough consistency.

## A worked example

A document platform has 40,000 users, 2 million documents and 300 teams. With RBAC, roles such as "finance editor in the EU"
would have to be created for each combination; with 3 departments, 4 regions and 3 access levels, that is more than 30 roles,
and the number grows with every exception. With ABAC, the policy is compact, but every service needs the user's department
and region kept up to date. With ReBAC, a document shared with a team is resolved by walking three relationships: document,
folder and team membership. For a sharing platform, ReBAC usually leaves the fewest duplicated rules.

## How not to duplicate rules

The most expensive mistake is for each service to implement its own check. The alternative is to centralize the decision: an
authorization service that other services query, or a policy engine (such as Open Policy Agent or Cedar) distributed as a
library with versioned policies. What does not work is mixing both: if one service decides on its own, the central policy
stops being the only source of truth.

## Common mistakes

- Creating a role for every exception instead of modelling the attribute or relationship that explains it.
- Trusting attributes nobody updates, such as the department of an employee who changed area six months ago.
- Deciding authorization in the client without rechecking it on the server.

## When it does not apply

If the application has three roles and no exceptions, RBAC is enough and nothing more is needed. The complexity of ABAC or
ReBAC is justified when rules depend on context or on hierarchies that change.

## Checklist

- [ ] How many exceptions does the policy have, and can they be expressed as attributes or relationships?
- [ ] Who is the source of truth for each attribute, and how often is it updated?
- [ ] Is the decision made in one place or in each service?
- [ ] Do the tests cover the exceptions, not only the basic roles?
