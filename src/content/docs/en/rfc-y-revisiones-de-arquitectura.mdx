# RFCs and architecture reviews without bureaucracy

A change that affects three teams should not be decided in a hallway or in a chat with forty messages. It
should not wait six weeks for a committee that meets once a month either. Between those two extremes sits a
simple format: the RFC, a written proposal with a deadline for comments and a recorded outcome.

Review processes usually fail for one of two reasons. Either they have no deadline, so proposals stay open
forever, or they have so many steps that nobody wants to go through them, even for a small change. A good
RFC fixes both problems at once.

## What an RFC is, and how it differs from an ADR

An RFC (request for comments) is the proposal document. It describes a problem, the options considered and a
recommendation, and it goes to review before anyone builds anything. An ADR is the record of the decision once
it has been made. The RFC answers "what are we proposing, and what do others think?"; the ADR answers "what did
we decide, and why?"

The natural flow has two linked steps: the RFC is reviewed and decided, and the decision is copied into an ADR
that links back to the RFC. Without the RFC, the ADR arrives with no context. Without the ADR, the RFC gets lost
in a channel's history.

## A flow with deadlines

The most practical model has three stages:

- **Draft.** The author describes the problem, at least two options and a recommendation. If the document runs
  past two or three pages, the problem is usually not well bounded yet.
- **Timeboxed review.** The proposal is published with a closing date, for example five working days. Comments
  can be informative or blocking. Only blocking comments stop the decision, and they must describe a concrete risk.
- **Decision.** At the close, the author or the person accountable for the decision summarises the comments,
  accepts or rejects the proposal, and records the outcome in an ADR.

The silence rule is the key piece: if nobody responds within the deadline, the proposal counts as accepted. That
forces reviewers to take part, and it stops proposals from drifting in limbo.

## Criteria for what goes to review

Not every change needs an RFC. A useful test is to ask whether the change meets any of these conditions: it affects
more than one team, it is hard to reverse, it touches a public contract or it commits a significant budget. If it
meets none, a note in the pull request is enough.

A worked example: a team wants to replace its message queue library. It affects three services, changes the message
format and the migration takes two months. It meets at least two of the criteria (it changes a contract between services and is hard to reverse), so it deserves an RFC. Upgrading a date
formatting library that is backwards compatible does not.

## Common mistakes

- **Reviewing without a deadline.** Proposals that stay open indefinitely are the fastest way to stall a team.
- **Turning review into a vote.** Review looks for risks, not for a majority. A blocking comment should rest on a
  fact, not only on a preference.
- **Writing the RFC after building.** A document that justifies something already built is a late record, not a proposal.
- **Forgetting the option of doing nothing.** An RFC that does not compare against leaving things as they are does not
  really compare anything.

## When it does not apply

An RFC has a cost. In a small team that sits together, a fifteen-minute conversation and a note in the ADR are enough.
It is also a poor fit for reversible decisions that would take a week to review, because the process would take longer
than the mistake. Bureaucracy appears when the same format is applied to everything.

## Checklist

- [ ] Does the proposal describe the problem before the solution?
- [ ] Are there at least two options, including doing nothing?
- [ ] Does it have a closing date, and is it clear who decides?
- [ ] Do blocking comments describe a concrete risk?
- [ ] Is the decision recorded in an ADR that links to the RFC?
