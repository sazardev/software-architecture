# Announcing changes to avoid collisions between teams

Imagine two teams touching the same module in the same week, each unaware of the other. The first
one finds the collision when merging: conflicts in lines it did not write and that nobody fully
understands. The second finds it later, when its fix undoes a change it did not know about. The
cost is not in the code. It is in the time someone spends reconstructing what happened and deciding
who gets to decide.

The thesis of this article is simple: most of these collisions are avoided with three very cheap
tools. The first is **clear ownership**: every area of the system has a visible owning team in the
repository. The second is an **intent notice**: a short message, with an expiry date, published
before touching a shared area. The third is **light, explicit reservations**: short-lived holds,
never open-ended. Two rules complement them: change contracts by adding before removing, and keep a
small, rule-based coordination channel.

## Where the collision comes from

The collision is a coordination problem, not a technical one. It appears when the boundary between
teams does not match the boundary in the code, so every change crosses lines nobody announces. The
[law of Conway](/docs/ley-de-conway-team-topologies#the-inverse-conway-maneuver) explains why that
mismatch tends to happen. Here we care about the daily cost, which usually shows up in three forms:

- **Merge conflicts.** Two branches edit the same lines. They are resolved by hand, with little
  context, and it is easy to pick the wrong version.
- **Changes that overwrite each other.** One team assumes a behaviour that the other has just
  changed. The error appears far from the change that caused it, and it takes time to locate.
- **Blind fixes.** Someone repairs a bug without knowing whether the behaviour was intentional. The
  "fix" undoes someone else's decision and the problem returns in another form.

The asymmetry of costs justifies the approach. A two-line notice costs minutes; the collision it
prevents can cost days of reconstruction. That is why the tools here are light: they do not try to
stop work, they make it visible in time.

## Clear ownership

An owned module answers a basic question: who decides. Without that answer, any change looks
allowed, and in practice everyone shares the review and nobody actually does it.

Three elements are enough to start:

- **An owners file**, versioned with the code, that maps each path to a team. On GitHub it is called
  `CODEOWNERS`; other platforms have equivalents. The shape matters more than the tool:

```text
/src/payments/        team-payments
/src/catalog/         team-catalog
/contracts/events/    team-orders  architecture
```

- **Mandatory review by owners** for changes in their paths. This way the notice reaches the people
  who can decide, not only the people who happen to be nearby.
- **An explicit owner for shared contracts** (schemas, APIs, file formats). This is where a
  collision costs the most, because the change affects consumers who are not in the conversation.
  The article on [event ownership](/docs/contratos-de-eventos-versionados#ownership-of-the-event)
  develops this.

A design criterion: the owner is a **team**, not a person. People change roles, and ownership
assigned to someone who has left leaves the area without a responsible party.

## Intent notices

A notice is a short, visible message published **before** starting a change that touches a shared
area. Its purpose is to let the other team react while doing so is still cheap. It answers five
questions:

| Field           | Question it answers                             |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| What            | What changes: behaviour, contract or structure? |
| Where           | Which paths, tables or contracts are touched?   |
| How long        | How long will the area be in flux?              |
| Who is affected | Which teams or consumers must react?            |
| Expires         | Until when is the notice valid?                 |

The last two fields are the ones usually missing, and they prevent the most typical problem: an old
notice that keeps blocking an area that is already free. A notice with an expiry can be ignored
without guilt once it lapses, and the team that published it must remove or renew it.

Illustrative example (an assumption, not a real case): the payments team announces that it will
change how amounts are rounded in `/src/payments/`, that it will take two days, that billing is
affected, and that the notice expires on Friday. With that information, billing can plan its work
instead of discovering the change in the next merge.

The notice goes somewhere everyone involved can read: a channel or an issue labelled by area, not a
private message. A private message does not coordinate anything, because nobody else hears about it.

## Light, explicit reservations

A reservation declares that an area is in use for a short period. It is not a technical lock and
does not block access to the repository: it is a visible agreement that the organization respects.
Its rules are few and deliberate:

- **Always with an end date.** An open-ended reservation becomes a bottleneck, and because nobody
  releases it, it ends up doing more harm than the problem it solved.
- **With an owner and a reason.** Anyone can ask about it and the owner answers. Without a reason,
  there is no way to judge whether it still needs to exist.
- **Released when the work ends.** And if it expires, the area is free again without asking.
- **Only when needed.** A reservation is the strongest of the three tools. Use it for work that
  cannot be mixed in, such as a data migration or the rewrite of a contract. For everything else, the
  notice is enough.

The word "light" matters. A lock prevents; a reservation coordinates. If reservations start being
used to prevent rather than coordinate, they begin to replace the conversation between teams, and
that is an organizational problem that a rule does not fix.

## When to announce and when not to

Announce when at least one of these conditions holds:

- The change crosses an ownership boundary, that is, it touches paths owned by another team.
- It changes a contract: an API, an event, a schema or a file format.
- It occupies a shared area for more than a day (an illustrative threshold, a judgement call).
- It is hard to reverse.

Do not announce when the change stays inside your own module, when it is a one-line fix with no
effect on other teams, or when it already goes through a review that notifies owners, such as an
[RFC](/docs/rfc-y-revisiones-de-arquitectura#criteria-for-what-goes-to-review). A change that goes
through an RFC is already announced in the RFC.

Excess notices are noise, and noise has a concrete effect: if everything is announced, nobody reads
the channel, and on the day the notice matters it gets lost among the others. A working rule that
helps: if the notice would not change what the other team does that week, it does not need to be
published.

## Changes that break contracts

When a change is incompatible, a notice is not enough: the way the change is made has to change.
The most widely used technique is **parallel change**, also called "expand and contract". Martin
Fowler describes it in 2014 with three phases
([Fowler, "ParallelChange"](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/ParallelChange.html)):

| Phase    | What happens                                                                   |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Expand   | The new form is added next to the old one. Both work for a while.              |
| Migrate  | Consumers move to the new form, one by one. This is usually the longest phase. |
| Contract | When nobody uses the old form any more, it is removed.                         |

The order is the key: first add, then move consumption, and only at the end remove. Removing before
migrating breaks whoever still reads the old form, and that is the most serious collision of all.

The migration phase needs a **deprecation notice**, which announces that the old form is going to be
retired. For HTTP APIs there is a standard header: `Deprecation`, defined in RFC 9745, indicates from
when the resource is deprecated without changing its behaviour. The `Sunset` header, which that same
RFC references, indicates when it will stop responding. For events there is no standard equivalent,
so the notice goes through the channel and through a consumption metric, as the article on
[versioned event contracts](/docs/contratos-de-eventos-versionados#deprecation-double-publishing-and-measured-retirement)
describes.

## A small channel with rules

Coordination needs a place, but it does not have to be a permanent meeting. A small channel with
rules is usually enough:

- **One channel per domain or per contract**, not one general channel for the whole company. A
  channel that everyone reads ends up read by no one.
- **A fixed format for notices**, with the five fields from the table above.
- **Who answers for each area** and within what time frame, agreed in writing.
- **Meetings only when needed.** They are called when two notices collide and cannot be settled in
  writing. They are not the default mechanism, because every meeting has a cost paid in everyone's
  time. This fits the [interaction modes](/docs/ley-de-conway-team-topologies#three-interaction-modes)
  of Team Topologies: intensive collaboration is reserved for what needs it.

## Signs that coordination is missing

There are recurring symptoms that show the tools above are not working:

- Repeated merge conflicts in the same paths.
- Two teams building the same thing, because neither knew the other was doing it.
- Reverts of other teams' changes made without talking to the owner of the area.
- Nobody knows whom to ask about a shared contract.

Our own judgement, not a rule: if one of these signs appears regularly over a quarter, first review
the ownership boundaries. Often the problem is not a lack of notices, but that the owner of an area
does not match the person who changes it.

## Common mistakes

- **Ownership by person, not by team.** When the person changes role, the area has no one
  responsible and changes start landing without review.
- **Notices without an expiry.** They pile up, block free areas and end up ignored.
- **Open-ended reservations.** They create bottlenecks that nobody dares to release.
- **Announcing after changing.** The notice no longer coordinates anything; it only reports what
  happened.
- **Removing before migrating.** It breaks consumers that still depend on the old form.
- **Turning everything into a meeting.** Teams stop writing their decisions down, and coordination
  depends on who happens to be in the room.

## Checklist

- [ ] Every shared path has an owning team, versioned in the repository.
- [ ] Changes in another team's paths require a review from its owner.
- [ ] Shared contracts have an explicit owner and a history.
- [ ] Each notice states what changes, where, how long, who is affected and when it expires.
- [ ] Every reservation has an owner, a reason and an end date.
- [ ] Contracts change by expand and contract, with a deprecation notice.
- [ ] There is a channel per domain with a notice format and a response time.
- [ ] Signs of missing coordination are reviewed periodically.
