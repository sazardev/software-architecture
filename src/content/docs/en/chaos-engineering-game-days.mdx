# Chaos engineering and game days: failing on purpose

A resilience mechanism is not proven until something really fails. A timeout that was written but
never measured, a retry nobody has seen in action, or a failover to another zone that only exists
in a diagram are hypotheses. The most expensive way to check them is to wait for a real incident.
**Chaos engineering** brings that moment forward: it causes small, controlled and observed faults,
so the team learns how the system reacts before production does it for them.

This article focuses on practice: how to design an experiment, which faults are worth injecting,
how it relates to SLOs, and what **game days** are, the planned exercises run with the teams. The
design patterns being tested (timeouts, retries, isolation) are covered in
[resilience in distributed systems](/docs/resiliencia-sistemas-distribuidos).

## What it is and what it is not

Chaos engineering is not breaking things at random in production. It is experimenting on a system
to build confidence in its ability to withstand turbulent conditions. An engineer states what
should happen, introduces a realistic disturbance and compares what happens with the prediction.
If they match, confidence goes up. If they do not, the team has found a defect at a moment it
chose.

Netflix popularized the term. The article "Chaos Engineering" by Basiri and colleagues, published
in _IEEE Software_ in 2016, describes its principles.

## The principles, in practice

The _Principles of Chaos Engineering_ site lists five advanced principles. They make a good
checklist for deciding whether an experiment is well designed:

| Principle                      | What it demands in practice                                                        |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Hypothesis around steady state | Define a measurable output of the system (error rate, latency, completed orders).  |
| Vary real-world events         | Inject what actually happens: a node dies, a dependency is slow, a zone goes down. |
| Run experiments in production  | Ideally in production, or in something as close to it as possible.                 |
| Automate experiments           | Run them continuously, not as an isolated event.                                   |
| Minimize blast radius          | Limit who is affected and be able to stop the experiment at once.                  |

The order matters. Without a hypothesis about steady state there is no experiment, only damage.
And the blast radius is not optional: starting small, with a stop button at hand, is what earns
permission to do more.

## Steady state, SLI, SLO and error budget

Steady state is not a feeling that "everything looks fine". It is expressed with the same
indicators operations uses: an SLI, such as the share of orders completed in under two seconds.
The [SLO and error budget](/docs/observabilidad-slo-presupuesto-error#slos-and-the-error-budget)
turn that indicator into a threshold and an amount of tolerable failure over a period.

That amount is what gives experiments their meaning. If the service has budget to spare, there is
room to inject a fault in production at low risk. If the budget is spent, the experiment moves to
a test environment or waits. This rule is the author's criterion, not a standard from any vendor:
what matters is agreeing on it beforehand, not arguing about it during an incident.

Without observability there is no possible experiment. If you cannot see the service's error rate
during the injection, you will not know whether the hypothesis held or when to stop.

## Fault types worth injecting

The most useful faults are the ones the system meets in real life. Each one answers a different
question:

- **Latency.** A dependency answers, but slowly. It is the most revealing fault: the system stays
  "up" while threads, queues and retries pile up. See
  [slow is worse than down](/docs/reintentos-con-jitter#slow-is-worse-than-down).
- **Dependency outage.** Does the service degrade gracefully when the payment, email or search
  provider stops responding, or do errors cascade?
- **Packet loss or network partition.** Lost packets or a cut between two services. It tests
  timeouts and reconnection logic.
- **Resource exhaustion.** Memory, CPU, file descriptors, database connections or disk. It often
  reveals limits that nobody configured.
- **Zone failure.** An entire availability zone is lost. It is the closest experiment to a serious
  incident and therefore the one that needs the most preparation.

Start with the first two. They look most like daily events and carry the least risk when the blast
radius is well bounded.

## Designing an experiment

A well-written experiment fits in a table. If you cannot fill in every row, it is not yet an
experiment:

| Field                   | Question it answers                                                    |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Hypothesis              | What must remain true during the fault, and on which metric?           |
| Metric                  | Which indicator checks it, and with what threshold?                    |
| Blast radius            | What share of traffic, which services and which customers are exposed? |
| Stop condition          | Which metric value stops the experiment automatically?                 |
| Rollback plan           | How is the fault removed, and what if the removal fails?               |
| Who is told and decides | Which owners are informed, who can stop it, and where is it announced? |
| Window and timing       | When does it run, who is on call, and with what expected load?         |

An illustrative example: "If the payment provider answers with 800 ms of extra latency on 5% of
requests, orders still complete in under two seconds for 99% of users. Stop if the checkout error
rate exceeds 0.5% for two minutes." The hypothesis is concrete, the metric exists, the stop is
automatic and the radius is a fraction of traffic.

Stop conditions must be real alarms, not a recommendation in a document. AWS Fault Injection
Service, for example, uses CloudWatch alarms as stop conditions and halts the experiment when they
fire; once stopped, it cannot be resumed. That property is a good
design test: stopping should be cheap and safe.

## Game days: rehearsing the people

A game day is a planned exercise in which real teams practise how they respond to a scenario. Chaos
engineering checks the system with small, continuous experiments. A game day checks **the people
and the procedures**: detection, communication, roles, runbooks and the decision to mitigate.

AWS best practices for game days (REL12-BP05, _Conduct game days regularly_) work well as a
checklist:

- Involve the teams that would respond in production, and business stakeholders too.
- Announce the date, time and scenario in advance to all participants and stakeholders, especially
  for the first one.
- Rehearse in replicas of the production environment, defined as infrastructure as code, when
  possible.
- If a negative impact appears, roll the exercise back and document the fix.
- Hold a post-exercise review and turn the findings into actions with an owner and a date.

Split the roles: someone controls the injection and can stop it, someone watches the metrics, the
response team works as if it were real, and an observer notes what is done and in what order. The
roles in a real incident are the same; their detailed treatment is in
[incident management and postmortems](/docs/gestion-incidentes-postmortems#roles-who-does-what-during-an-incident).

### Game day versus recovery drill

They are similar exercises with different goals. A **disaster recovery drill** measures whether a
service or a region can be recovered within an RTO and an RPO, and it is usually less frequent and
broader in scope (see [recovery testing](/docs/regiones-zonas-y-recuperacion#recovery-testing)). A
game day rehearses the organizational response to a scenario, is short, and has communication and
decision goals. Continuous chaos engineering is neither: it is a set of small experiments running
alongside normal work, not an event.

## Tools

Choose the tool by where the workload runs, not by popularity:

- **AWS Fault Injection Service** is a managed service for workloads on AWS, with CloudWatch-based
  stop conditions as described above.
- **LitmusChaos** and **Chaos Mesh** are open-source projects for Kubernetes. LitmusChaos moved to
  _incubating_ in the CNCF in January 2022. The current status of both projects changes: check
  cncf.io/projects before adopting either.

No tool replaces the discipline of hypothesis, blast radius and stop. A tool without those three
things only produces incidents with more steps.

## When it makes sense and when it does not

**It makes sense** when steady state can be measured, observability shows the impact almost in real
time, rollback procedures exist and the owners of the affected services have agreed with informed
consent.

**It does not** when the system has no reasonable timeouts or retries. Injecting a fault to discover
that there is no timeout is a design review in disguise: the fix should come first. Nor does it
replace load testing, contract tests or architecture review. And it is not done without
observability: an experiment run blind teaches nothing.

## Common mistakes

- **Experimenting without a hypothesis.** "Let's see what happens" produces noise, not conclusions.
- **Starting with all the traffic.** The first experiment should limit impact to a small fraction
  and one service, not the whole platform.
- **Untested stop button.** If nobody has checked that the stop works in a rehearsed scenario, it
  does not exist.
- **Game days without the owners.** Running them behind the teams' backs breeds distrust and,
  rightly, rejection.
- **Looking for culprits.** A finding that is punished stops being reported. The target is the
  system and the process, in line with the blameless culture of the
  [postmortem](/docs/gestion-incidentes-postmortems#blame-human-error-and-a-blameless-culture).
- **Not closing the loop.** A finding without an action, owner and date repeats in the next
  incident. See [from actions to real improvement](/docs/gestion-incidentes-postmortems#from-actions-to-real-improvement).

## Checklist

- [ ] Each experiment has a hypothesis about a steady-state metric and a threshold.
- [ ] The metric and its associated SLI are instrumented before anything is injected.
- [ ] The blast radius is bounded and documented, starting with a small fraction.
- [ ] An automatic stop condition exists and someone can stop the experiment by hand.
- [ ] The rollback plan has been rehearsed and the owners of the affected services are informed.
- [ ] The error budget allows the experiment; if not, it moves to a test environment.
- [ ] Game days have a scenario, roles, an observer and a post-exercise review with actions.
- [ ] Findings are recorded and turned into design improvements, not only into alerts.
