# Case study: a system from start to finish

Standalone articles explain one decision each: when to choose a modular monolith, how to store
data, what to do with a saga. In a real project the decisions chain together. The choice of
database shapes how you guarantee that a seat is not sold twice; that guarantee shapes the
integration; the integration shapes what can be deployed separately. This case walks through the
order in which those decisions are worth taking and writes down why each one is taken.

<Callout tone="info" title="Illustrative case, not a real system">
  The system is invented to explain the reasoning. The requirement figures are **assumptions** and
  are labelled as such; they are not measurements from any project. Replace them with your own
  context before using this walkthrough as a template.
</Callout>

## The system and its requirements

A chain of gyms wants members to book a place in classes (yoga, spinning, strength sessions) from
a mobile app and from the web. Each class has a capacity, some are paid with a pass of sessions,
and all send a confirmation and a reminder. The domain is kept narrow on purpose: there is no shop,
no trainer payroll, no turnstile access control. These are pieces a reader would expect to see and
that are left out here so the example does not scatter.

### Functional requirements

- See a centre's class timetable and book a free place.
- Cancel a booking within the allowed window and get the place back.
- Charge the booking to a pass when the member has no place included in their subscription.
- Send confirmation and reminders by email and push notification.
- Show the member's booking history.

### Quality attributes

Non-functional requirements weigh more than functional ones when decisions are made. The table
separates what comes from the business from design assumptions the team must validate.

| Attribute             | Target                                      | Source               |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | -------------------- |
| Overselling seats     | Zero                                        | Business requirement |
| Booking availability  | 99.9 % per month                            | **Assumption**       |
| Booking latency (p95) | Under 400 ms                                | **Assumption**       |
| Booking confirmation  | Under 2 minutes (p95)                       | **Assumption**       |
| Volume                | 120 centres, 300,000 active members         | **Assumption**       |
| Peak                  | 1,500 bookings per minute at 7:00           | **Assumption**       |
| Growth                | Three times the volume within twelve months | **Assumption**       |
| Team size             | Six engineers and one half-time architect   | **Assumption**       |

The zero-overselling rule weighs the most. An availability failure is annoying; a seat sold twice
creates complaints and loss of trust. So integrity is treated as a hard requirement and performance
as a target to measure. The criteria for choosing the attributes that drive decisions are in
[quality attributes](/docs/atributos-de-calidad).

## Domain and boundaries

Before choosing any style, the contexts are drawn. A bounded context is an area where a term means
one specific thing. This system has four:

- **Catalog**: classes, timetables, capacity and cancellation rules.
- **Bookings**: a member's place in a class. This is the core of the system.
- **Passes and billing**: session balances, charges and the relationship with the payment gateway.
- **Notifications**: templates, channels and deliveries. It knows nothing about places.

Identity (who the member is and what they may do) is bought from an identity provider rather than
built. The boundary between Bookings and Catalog causes the most debate: if capacity lives in
Catalog, Bookings must ask it on every operation, and that puts a call on the critical path. The
decision is that Bookings keeps its own copy of capacity, updated by events from the catalogue, and
accepts that the copy may lag by seconds. The Bookings context does not contain the charging logic;
it only knows that a booking needs a valid pass.

The method for reaching these contexts, with ubiquitous language and context maps, is in
[Domain-Driven Design](/docs/domain-driven-design).

## Starting style: modular monolith

The first style decision is the easiest to get wrong. With a team of six and a domain that is still
changing, one deployment per service adds network, operational and coordination cost that buys
nothing in the first phase. Martin Fowler makes this case in _MonolithFirst_ (2015): almost all the
successful microservice stories started as a monolith that grew too large, and it is hard to draw
good boundaries before you know the business. It is an opinion with limited evidence, and it should
be read that way.

The decision is a **modular monolith**: one process and one database, with each context as a module
that has a public API and rules that prevent importing another module's internals. The rules are
checked in CI, not by good intentions. The procedure is in
[modular monolith first](/docs/monolito-modular-primero#why-start-with-a-modular-monolith), and the
ways to enforce boundaries are in
[monolith versus microservices](/docs/monolito-vs-microservicios#enforcing-boundaries-in-a-modular-monolith).

The alternative rejected here is **microservices from day one**, one service per context. It is
rejected because the boundaries are still hypotheses and each boundary mistake costs a network
contract rather than a refactor. The risk of the modular monolith is the opposite: modules coupling
over time. It is mitigated with dependency control, not with more services.

```text
src/
  modules/
    catalog/   api.ts  internal/
    bookings/  api.ts  internal/
    billing/   api.ts  internal/
    notifications/  api.ts  internal/
```

### When the first service is split out

The monolith is not forever. A module is extracted when the reason to separate it can be named and
measured. The candidate here is **Notifications**, for reasons unrelated to the rest of the business:
it has a different load profile (bulk sends spike at 19:00, assumption), it depends on external providers that
fail in different ways, and its consistency is eventual by nature. If it stays inside, a slow
provider lengthens booking transactions.

The trigger rule the team commits to review each quarter is that at least two of these conditions
hold: the module needs to scale independently, its release cycle differs from the rest, or one of
its failures has already affected bookings more than once. The conditions and extraction order are
discussed in [migration paths](/docs/monolito-vs-microservicios#migration-paths).

The first service out is Notifications, in month four. It is the only boundary that crosses the
network in this version, and the article's figure marks it on the map.

## Data model and stores

The monolith has one relational database. The reason is not ideological: booking is an operation
that needs integrity, and a relational store provides it out of the box. The queries that justify
the store are listed before choosing it, following
[choosing a database from the queries](/docs/persistencia-politica-elegir-bd#start-with-the-list-of-queries).

There are three ways to prevent overselling. The first is raising the isolation level to
serializable and retrying transactions that fail. The second is locking the class row with
`SELECT ... FOR UPDATE`. The third is decrementing capacity with a single conditional statement: an
`UPDATE` on the class with the condition that at least one place remains, and checking that it
affected one row. The third is chosen. It is the simplest, and the database guarantees the
statement's atomicity without the application having to reason about concurrency.

It is worth being precise about what PostgreSQL guarantees by default, because the example depends
on it. According to its documentation, **Read Committed** is the default level, and **Repeatable
Read** is implemented as _snapshot isolation_, which prevents dirty, non-repeatable and phantom
reads but still allows serialization anomalies such as write skew. The conditional statement does
not need a higher level for this specific case, but a higher level is not a universal guarantee
either. The isolation reasoning is in
[ACID data and isolation](/docs/datos-acid-y-aislamiento#transactions-and-acid).

Each context's data lives in the same schema, with tables separated by module and no foreign keys
crossing the boundaries of future contexts. That discipline is what will allow Notifications to be
extracted without migrating data blind. For booking history a normal table with an index on member
and date is used. No specialised store is needed yet, and a document store is rejected because the
queries are few and relational.

## Integration: calls, events and sagas

There are three kinds of communication, and each has its place:

- **Synchronous call** from the app to the Bookings API, because the member waits for a response.
- **Synchronous call** from Bookings to Passes, and from Passes to the payment gateway, because the
  charge must resolve before the place is confirmed.
- **Asynchronous event** from Bookings to Notifications, because sending an email must not delay the
  member or make a booking fail.

### Booking with a charge: an orchestrated saga

Booking with a pass involves three steps that are not in the same database when the charge goes out
to the gateway: reserve the place, charge the pass and confirm. If the charge fails after the place
was reserved, the place must be released. That is a **saga**: a sequence of local transactions with
compensations. The alternative, a two-phase commit between the database and the gateway, is rejected
because the gateway does not take part in that protocol, and holding resources while waiting for the
provider penalises latency.

The saga is orchestrated: the Bookings module coordinates the steps and decides what to compensate.
It is preferred over choreography, in which each module reacts to the others' events, because this
flow has only three steps and the compensation logic should live in one place where it can be read.
Step types, compensation and its limits are in
[sagas, outbox and idempotency](/docs/sagas-outbox-idempotencia#sagas-local-transactions-with-compensation).

The call to the gateway carries an idempotency key. Stripe documents the same mechanism: the key is
sent in a header, the platform stores the first response and returns it on retries, and keys of API v1 may be
pruned after at least 24 hours
([Stripe documentation](https://docs.stripe.com/api/idempotent_requests)). We apply the same approach
to our booking API: the app generates a key per booking attempt, so a retry after a network drop does
not create two places. The detail on keys in APIs is in
[idempotency](/docs/sagas-outbox-idempotencia#idempotency-keys-in-apis).

### Outbox at the boundary

When Bookings confirms a place it must publish the `BookingConfirmed` event so that Notifications
sends the email. If it writes to the database and then publishes to the broker, a failure between
the two steps leaves data without an event or events without data. The solution is the **outbox
table**: the event is stored in the same transaction as the booking, and a separate process forwards
it to the broker. Microservices.io describes the pattern as the answer to the dual-write problem,
with two ways of relaying: following the transaction log or polling the table
([transactional outbox](https://microservices.io/patterns/data/transactional-outbox.html)).

Relaying can repeat messages after a restart, so the consumer must be idempotent. Here, Notifications
stores the identifier of each event it has already processed. The outbox is introduced at the moment
of extraction and not before: inside the monolith, Bookings and the sender can live with an in-memory
event, because nothing crosses the network. The cost of adding the table is low, so it goes in
together with the first real boundary.

## Caching and queues

The class catalogue is read far more often than it changes. A cache with a short TTL (30 seconds, design assumption) on
timetable reads reduces database load at peak. Invalidation is the hard problem: the key is
invalidated **after** the transaction that changed the capacity commits, never before, so that a
concurrent read does not store the old value again. The short TTL is the safety net for whatever is
forgotten. Strategies and their traps are in
[caching, invalidation and stampedes](/docs/caching-colas-streaming#caching-buying-latency-with-freshness).

Notifications go through a queue with competing consumers, a dead-letter queue for failed messages,
and retries with growing delays and random variation. Retries without variation produce storms when
a provider comes back. The retry mechanism and its budgets are in
[resilience in distributed systems](/docs/resiliencia-sistemas-distribuidos#retries-useful-selfish-and-dangerous-in-chains).

A partitioned log is not used yet. The volume of the confirmation event does not justify retaining
history for reprocessing, and a queue is enough. If the team later needs to rebuild projections or
reprocess a month of events, the decision is reopened; that is one of the changes in the evolution
described below.

## Security and identity

The member authenticates with an identity provider that implements OpenID Connect; the app uses the
authorization code flow with PKCE and does not store passwords. The OAuth 2.0 security best current
practice (RFC 9700, published in 2025 as BCP 240) deprecates some modes of operation that are
considered less secure, so the team takes it as the baseline for configuration
([RFC 9700](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9700)). Access tokens are short-lived, and app sessions
are revoked from the provider.

Authorization is finer than a role. A member can only book at a centre their subscription belongs to,
and can only cancel their own bookings. A "member" role alone is not enough; the decision combines the
role with membership. The model and its variants are in
[RBAC, ABAC and ReBAC](/docs/autorizacion-rbac-abac-rebac#rbac-the-role-as-the-source-of-truth).

Personal data (name, email, booking history) is minimised: the Notifications module receives only an
identifier and a channel, and resolves the name at send time through Identity. So deleting a member
does not require hunting for copies in the notification queue. The criteria and GDPR obligations are
in [privacy by design](/docs/privacidad-por-diseno-gdpr).

## Observability and SLOs

The question the team must be able to answer is whether a member's booking was fast and correct. It
is measured with two indicators taken from the user's point of view, not from the servers. The first
is the percentage of bookings that respond in under 400 ms. The second is the percentage of
confirmations that arrive in under 2 minutes. Both are assumptions and are revisited with real data
in the first month.

The availability SLO for booking is 99.9 % per month (assumption), and the error budget comes from that target. Google
defines the SLI as a carefully defined quantitative measure and the SLO as a target value for that
measure ([SRE, Service Level Objectives](https://sre.google/sre-book/service-level-objectives/)). The
team decides the budget policy: while there is margin, it deploys; if the budget runs out, risky work
stops and reliability takes priority. That policy and alerts based on budget burn are in
[SLOs and the error budget](/docs/observabilidad-slo-presupuesto-error#slos-and-the-error-budget).

Traces cross the modules and the queue with the same correlation identifier. Without it, the case of
"the booking was charged but the email did not arrive" means cross-checking three dashboards by hand.

## Deployment and progressive delivery

The monolith deploys as one unit, but each behaviour change ships behind a feature flag. The new
timetable screen is switched on first for the internal team, then for a pilot centre, and then for
everyone. That separation between deploying and exposing is the basis of progressive delivery. For
bookings, the critical part, a canary is used in steps: 5 %, 25 % and 100 % of traffic, with SLI
checks at each step and automatic rollback if it drops below target. The procedure is in
[flags and canaries](/docs/entrega-progresiva-flags-canary#canary-exposing-in-steps).

Schema changes follow the expand-and-contract pattern, with three deployments: add the new column,
copy and read in parallel, and remove the old one once nothing uses it. The migration of the seats
table, the most sensitive one, runs in low-traffic windows with batched backfill. The phases are in
[migrations without downtime](/docs/migraciones-bd-sin-downtime#the-four-phases).

## One year later: when requirements change

The initial architecture is checked against reality at twelve months. We assume three changes the
business really asks for:

1. **Waiting list per class.** A new feature reclaims released places. It is implemented inside
   Bookings, because it shares the same capacity rule. It is not extracted: the integrity rule must
   not cross the network.
2. **Passes per centre.** The charge changes shape: a member can have different balances per centre.
   It affects the data model of Passes, and the change is made with expand-and-contract, without
   touching event consumers.
3. **Gateway change.** The payment provider is replaced. The call is isolated behind an adapter inside
   Passes, so the change does not reach Bookings or the app.

The `BookingConfirmed` event gains a new field, `channel`, which Notifications uses to choose push or
email. A change like this is safe if it does not break existing consumers, and contract versioning
defines which changes are ([safe changes in event contracts](/docs/contratos-de-eventos-versionados#which-changes-are-safe)).

With three times the volume, the 7:00 peak rises from 1,500 to about 4,500 bookings per minute
(assumption). At that point the timetable cache is no longer enough, and the team decides whether the
partitioned log is worth its cost. The decision is not taken on intuition: queue consumption is
measured at the first real peak and compared with the target. If it falls short, the first candidate
to change is the notification queue, not the bookings core.

## Summary ADR

Decisions that change the shape of the system are written as ADRs. Nygard's 2011 format (title,
context, decision, status and consequences) remains the basis of most templates
([Documenting Architecture Decisions](https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions)).
This is the ADR for the first boundary, in summary form:

**ADR-007: Notifications is extracted as the first service in month four**

- **Status:** accepted. Quarterly review.
- **Context:** bulk sends degrade booking transactions; the email provider fails more often than the
  rest of the system.
- **Decision:** extract Notifications with its own queue and an outbox in Bookings. The rest of the
  monolith does not change.
- **Consequences:** the system's first network contract appears, and its versioning. Fault isolation
  and independent scaling are gained. A relay process is added that must be operated.
- **Review criterion:** if queue consumption misses the target at the first peak, or if Notifications
  stops having a different load profile, the boundary is reconsidered.

The format and lifecycle of ADRs are in [ADRs](/docs/adr#what-an-adr-is-and-what-problem-it-solves).

## When it applies and when it does not

This walkthrough is not a template for every system. With a two-person team and a single centre,
almost all the distribution decisions are unnecessary: a monolith with one database and an events
table solves the case. The integration pieces (saga, outbox, queue, stepped canary) are justified when
there are several real boundaries, different loads, or failure costs the business can name. Before
adding them, the question is which concrete requirement stops being met without them.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting from technology.** Choosing queues, Kubernetes and distributed databases before having the
  list of queries and requirements. The result is complexity with no requirement behind it.
- **Figures without labels.** Presenting an assumption as if it were a business requirement. In a later
  decision nobody will know what to review.
- **Boundaries by technical layer.** Splitting "API", "logic" and "data" into services instead of
  business contexts. Every request crosses three networks and no boundary protects anything.
- **Saga without thought-through compensation.** A flow with steps that cannot be undone and no decision
  about what happens if the charge fails halfway.
- **Cache without an invalidation plan.** A long TTL "so it is fast" that shows sold places as free for
  minutes.
- **Decisions that are not written down.** A year later nobody knows why the monolith has those
  boundaries, and closed discussions get reopened.

## Checklist

- [ ] Quality requirements have figures, and assumed figures are labelled as such.
- [ ] Each bounded context has an owner, its own language and boundary rules checked in CI.
- [ ] The first extraction has a nameable reason and a review criterion.
- [ ] Critical integrity (capacity, charge) is solved inside one transaction or a written saga.
- [ ] Every published event leaves through an outbox, and every consumer is idempotent.
- [ ] Caches have a TTL and an invalidation rule that runs after commit.
- [ ] There are SLI, SLO and an error budget before the first production deployment.
- [ ] Schema and contract changes follow expand-and-contract, without breaking changes.
- [ ] Each relevant decision has its ADR, with the rejected alternative and a review criterion.
